Short-paid by £11,500? Check one date before you accept it.

Most subcontractors take the cut and move on. Often they didn't have to.

The example below is a typical case with illustrative figures, not a real client.

A drylining subcontractor sends his monthly application for £38,400. The final date for payment comes and goes. Then £26,900 lands in the bank, with an email: "We've valued your works lower this month."

He's annoyed, but he needs the main contractor for the next job. So he accepts it and moves on.

He may have been owed the full £38,400. Here's why.

The short version: if the main contractor wants to pay you less than the notified sum, it must send a valid pay less notice before the deadline. If it doesn't, it generally has to pay the notified sum in full, and argue about the value later.

The four dates that decide what you get paid

  1. Due date. When the payment becomes due, set by your subcontract (or by the Scheme for Construction Contracts if the subcontract doesn't comply).
  2. Payment notice. The main contractor should state what it considers due, and how it worked it out, within 5 days of the due date. If it doesn't, your own application can become the notified sum, depending on your contract (or you can serve a default notice).
  3. Pay less notice deadline. If it wants to pay less than the notified sum, it must say so in writing, with its reasons, by the deadline in your contract. Where the Scheme applies, that's 7 days before the final date for payment.
  4. Final date for payment. The last day the money can arrive.

In our example, there was no payment notice and no pay less notice, just an email after the deadline. On most subcontracts, that email arrives too late to reduce the payment.

Why main contractors miss the deadline

Not usually on purpose. Their QS is valuing 30 subcontractors, the client's payment is late, and the valuation goes out when it goes out. But the Construction Act doesn't care why. The date is the date.

What to do if you've been cut

  1. Work out the dates. Put your due date and payment terms into the free Payment Notice Deadline Calculator.
  2. Check what you received. Was there a payment notice? A pay less notice? When exactly did it arrive, and does it explain how the figure was calculated?
  3. Write, don't phone. If no valid notice arrived in time, send a short, polite letter asking for the notified sum by a set date.
  4. Know your next steps. The Act gives you the right to suspend work after 7 days' notice, and to refer the dispute to adjudication. Take legal advice before using either.
  5. Fix next month. Send every application on time, in the right form, to the right person, so it counts if they miss their notice.

One more thing

Paying the notified sum doesn't end the argument about value. The main contractor can still dispute what the work is worth later. But it has to pay first, and that makes a big difference to your cash flow.
